Get Started What OpenVox Is

Ansible, Salt, and Chef are all capable tools, and plenty of shops run them well. OpenVox makes a different set of bets, and if they match how you think about infrastructure, nothing else comes close.

With OpenVox you describe the end state: this package installed, this service running, this file with these permissions. The platform figures out how to get there on any supported OS, and the same code that builds a system also verifies it. You maintain a model of your infrastructure, not a pile of scripts that happened to work last time. Push-based tools apply changes when someone runs them. The OpenVox agent checks in on a schedule, corrects drift automatically, and reports what changed. When someone hand-edits a config at 2 a.m., it gets fixed at the next run, and you have a record that it happened. At ten nodes that's a convenience. At ten thousand, it's the difference between knowing your fleet's state and hoping. The declarative DSL gives you typed parameters, dependency graphs, and reusable classes and defined types. Roles and profiles scale from one admin to large teams without your codebase collapsing into copy-paste. You get the structure of software engineering applied to infrastructure, because that's what it is. Thousands of battle-tested Forge modules, decades of accumulated operational knowledge, and tooling for testing, linting, and CI that treats infrastructure code like code. Vox Pupuli alone maintains well over a hundred actively tested modules, now validated against OpenVox itself. Chef and Puppet both ended up owned by companies with their own priorities. OpenVox can't be acquired, sunset, or relicensed out from under you. Its future belongs to its users, which means it has one.
